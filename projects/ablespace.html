01 — The Problem

Build to spec, not to taste.

AbleSpace was delivered as a job assessment: a Figma design and a feature list, with the expectation of a working, deployed app rather than a prototype. The constraint wasn't "what should this app do" — it was "match this spec exactly, on a deadline, with production-quality auth and access control."

02 — Product

Board, list, and everything a task needs.

Tasks support subtasks, comments, filtering and search, viewable as either a board or a list. The scope matched a real internal tool more than a to-do app — multi-user, permissioned, and built to hold up under actual project use.

03 — Architecture

Next.js App Router talking to a NestJS API.

The frontend is a Next.js App Router application; the backend is a separate NestJS service exposing a typed REST API, with MongoDB as the store. Splitting frontend and backend into independently deployable services (Vercel and Render, respectively) kept the assessment closer to how a real product team would ship it.

04 — Authentication & Authorization

Three ways in

JWT-based email login, Google OAuth, and a guest-login path for quick evaluation without creating an account.

Guarded access

NestJS guards enforce user-scoped access on every route, and DTO validation rejects malformed requests before they reach business logic — so a user can only ever see and edit their own boards and tasks.

05 — UX

Drag, drop, done.

A drag-and-drop Kanban board sits alongside a list view for the same data, with light and dark themes built in from the start rather than bolted on. Matching the Figma spec's spacing and component states — not just its rough layout — was most of the visual QA effort.

06 — Deployment & Engineering Decisions

Shipped clean.

The frontend deploys to Vercel and the backend to Render, both wired to auto-deploy on push. The build ships with zero lint errors — a deliberate bar for an assessment meant to demonstrate production readiness, not just feature completeness.

07 — Lessons Learned

Working from someone else's Figma file — rather than my own design instincts — was the real skill being tested. It meant resolving ambiguity (missing states, edge cases the design didn't cover) by inferring intent from the rest of the spec, instead of just defaulting to what I'd normally build.
